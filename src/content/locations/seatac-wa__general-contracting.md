## Why SeaTac Properties Need Thoughtful Renovation Planning

A lot of SeaTac's housing stock went up during the decades of airport expansion, which means you'll find a mix of 1960s and 1970s ranch and split-level homes alongside newer infill construction near the commercial corridors. Older homes in that range often have single-pane windows, thin wall insulation, and electrical panels that were never sized for modern kitchen or bathroom loads. When we open walls for a remodel, we routinely find aluminum branch wiring or undersized vent stacks that need correcting before new finishes go in, not after.

The region's clay-heavy soils and high water table also play a role. Additions and garage conversions need grading and drainage planning that a lot of general, non-local contractors skip, and that oversight shows up later as moisture intrusion at the slab edge or crawlspace. Any renovation that touches the building envelope here should account for Western Washington's wet season, not just the design on paper.

## Permitting and Code Considerations in SeaTac

Because of the airport's influence on local development, parts of SeaTac fall within noise-impact overlays that have, over the years, qualified older homes for insulation and window upgrade assistance. If your home is in one of those areas, it's worth checking before you finalize a remodel scope, since window and wall assembly upgrades done for noise reasons can often be combined with a kitchen or bathroom renovation to avoid opening the same wall twice. Our team flags this during the initial walkthrough rather than after demo has already started.

Our WA State Department of Labor & Industries General Contractor registration (#NATIORC792M6) covers the licensing side of this work, and we're IICRC and EPA certified for any remediation steps that overlap with a renovation, such as lead-safe work practices in homes built before 1978.

## Local note

One detail that catches homeowners off guard: SeaTac's older ranch-style homes frequently have crawlspace vapor barriers that were installed decades ago and have since torn or settled. When we're in for a kitchen or main-floor bathroom remodel, we check the crawlspace condition as a matter of course, because moisture working up through a failed vapor barrier can undermine new flooring or cabinetry within a couple of seasons if it's not addressed during the same project.

If your renovation is being driven by storm, fire, or water damage rather than a planned upgrade, the sequencing matters even more. We handle the mitigation and reconstruction phases as one continuous project, scoped once and documented once for your insurance carrier, rather than treating the rebuild as a separate job that starts from scratch after the drying crew leaves.
