## Do I call a restoration company or a plumber first?

Call whoever stops the water before you call whoever cleans it up. If a supply line, water heater, or appliance is still actively leaking and you can safely reach the shutoff valve, close it yourself. If you can't locate it or the leak is from a main line, call a plumber or your water utility to kill the source first.

Once the water is stopped (or if it's already stopped, like a one-time storm surge or a crawl space that flooded overnight), the next call is a restoration company, not a general handyman. Standing water left more than 24 to 48 hours starts breaking down drywall, warping subfloor, and creating conditions for mold growth, per the [EPA's guidance on flooded homes](https://www.epa.gov/flooded-homes). Drying a Gig Harbor crawl space properly, given the ambient moisture already in the ground here, usually requires commercial dehumidifiers and air movers run to the ANSI/IICRC S500 water damage restoration standard, not a box fan from the garage.

If the water involves downed power lines, a gas smell, or standing water near an electrical panel, call 911 or your utility company first. No restoration crew enters an active electrical or gas hazard.

## Should I call my insurance company before the water is removed?

No. Call the restoration company first, then notify your insurer once mitigation is underway. Most homeowners policies require you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage, and waiting days for an adjuster appointment while water sits in your crawl space works against you, not for you.

A documented, fast start to mitigation is also what protects the claim. The [Insurance Information Institute's guide to settling claims after a disaster](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster) notes that photo documentation, moisture readings, and an itemized scope of work are what insurers actually want to see. NARESTCO documents moisture levels and affected materials from the first visit, which gives you a paper trail if you end up filing a claim, whether the damage came from a burst pipe, a failed crawl space vapor barrier, or wind-driven rain off the Sound.

If you're not sure whether your policy covers the type of flooding you have (storm surge and surface water from heavy rain are often excluded under a standard homeowners policy and require separate flood coverage), that's a conversation for your agent, not something to sort out standing in a wet hallway. ## What should I do while I'm waiting for someone to arrive?

Move what you can off the wet floor, and don't run electrical equipment in standing water. If it's safe, open crawl space vents and interior doors to start some natural airflow, but don't attempt to run a shop vac on water that's near outlets or a panel.

Take photos of the water line on walls, any visibly soaked flooring, and the source if you can identify it (a split hose, a corroded fitting, a window that let in wind-driven rain). These photos become part of the documentation an adjuster will eventually want, and they help the technician understand the timeline when they arrive.
