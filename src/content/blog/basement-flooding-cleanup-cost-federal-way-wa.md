If you just found standing water in your basement after a heavy Puget Sound rain, or a floor drain backed up overnight near Dash Point or Twin Lakes, the first question is usually cost, not cause. Basement flooding cleanup in Federal Way typically runs from around $1,500 for a small unfinished basement with a few inches of clean water, up to $20,000 or more for a finished basement with sewage contamination and full demolition. The real number depends on how deep the water got, what the basement is made of, and what kind of water it was.

## How much does basement flooding cleanup cost in Federal Way, WA?

Most basement flood cleanups in Federal Way fall between $1,500 and $15,000, with the low end covering a quick extraction and dry-out and the high end covering demolition, antimicrobial treatment, and partial rebuild. Daylight basements common in Dash Point and Steel Lake, where part of the foundation sits above grade, often flood from a different source (gutter overflow, grading) than true below-grade basements, which more often take on groundwater through the foundation itself.

## What drives the price of basement flood cleanup up or down?

The biggest cost drivers are water depth, how much of the basement is finished, and the category of water involved. A couple inches on bare concrete costs far less to dry than six inches soaking into drywall, baseboards, and carpet pad.

Finish level matters because porous materials, carpet, carpet pad, drywall below the waterline, baseboards, and sometimes insulation, usually get cut out and replaced rather than dried in place once they've been sitting in water for more than a day or two. An unfinished basement with a bare slab and exposed framing is the cheapest scenario because there's little to remove.

Water category changes the scope significantly. Clean water from a supply line is handled differently than gray water from a washing machine discharge, and both are handled very differently from sewage. Any water that has come through a floor drain or sewer line from beyond the trap is Category 3, grossly contaminated water, and it requires antimicrobial treatment, PPE, and typically more aggressive material removal under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). That adds labor and disposal cost that a clean-water loss doesn't carry.

Other factors: basement square footage, how long the water sat before anyone called for help (mold risk climbs fast after 24-48 hours), and whether the basement has finished ceilings or electrical in the walls that need to come down for access.

## Does homeowners insurance cover a flooded basement?

It depends entirely on where the water came from. A standard homeowners policy typically covers sudden, accidental water damage from inside the home, like a burst supply line or a water heater that fails, but it usually does not cover flooding from outside groundwater or surface water.

Groundwater seeping through a foundation wall or a floor that floods from rising water outside the home is generally treated as a flood, which requires a separate flood policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) or a private flood carrier, not a standard homeowners policy. A sewer or drain backup, and a sump pump that overflows or fails, are usually excluded too unless you've added a specific water backup endorsement to your policy. For a side-by-side look at what's typically covered versus excluded across water losses generally (not basement-specific), see our [guide to homeowners insurance and water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) and the [Insurance Information Institute's breakdown of disaster coverage](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance). Coverage details vary by insurer and state, so check your actual policy language before assuming either way.

## What does the basement cleanup process actually involve?

Basement cleanup generally moves through five phases, regardless of who performs the work:

1. **Extraction.** Standing water is pumped and vacuumed out with truck-mounted or portable extraction equipment.
2. **Assessment and moisture mapping.** A moisture meter checks how far water traveled into walls, subfloor, and framing, since visible water is rarely the whole story.
3. **Controlled demolition.** Carpet, pad, and wet drywall below the waterline are cut out and removed, especially for Category 2 or 3 water where materials can't be safely dried in place.
4. **Drying and antimicrobial treatment.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run until moisture readings return to a dry standard; contaminated areas get antimicrobial treatment first.
5. **Reconstruction.** New drywall, baseboards, and flooring go back in once the space is confirmed dry.

This same general sequence applies whether the trigger was a storm, a failed sump pump, or a backed-up line.
