## How Much Does Flood Damage Restoration Cost in Federal Way, WA?

Most Federal Way flood cleanups run between $1,500 for a shallow crawl space intrusion caught early and $40,000 or more for a whole-floor flood that requires demolition and rebuild. The biggest swing factor isn't square footage alone, it's whether the water sat long enough to contaminate materials and whether drywall, insulation, and flooring have to come out.

## What Drives the Price Up or Down?

The water's category and how many days it takes to dry the structure move the price more than almost anything else. A gray-water sump backup caught the same day costs far less than storm water that sat in a crawl space for a week.

A few specific factors do most of the work:

- **Category of water.** Gray water from a failed sump pump or groundwater seepage is cheaper to handle than black water (sewage, flowing storm water from outside), which requires more material removal and antimicrobial treatment under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/).
- **Standing water depth and duration.** Water that sat for more than 24 to 48 hours is far more likely to have reached insulation, subflooring, and framing, which pushes the job from drying into demolition.
- **Square footage and layout.** A single crawl space zone is a day or two of equipment. A finished basement with multiple rooms, built-ins, and electrical in the walls takes longer and costs more.
- **Drying days.** Air movers and dehumidifiers are billed per day until the structure reads dry on a moisture meter. Older Federal Way ramblers with original vapor barriers sometimes dry slower than newer construction.
- **Material removal and rebuild.** Carpet and pad almost always come out after a flood. Drywall, baseboard, and insulation come out when they've wicked water above a few inches. Rebuild cost depends on finish level.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Flood Damage?

Standard homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage from rising water, storm surge, or surface water flowing in from outside. That kind of loss needs a separate flood policy, most commonly through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance), because homeowners policies specifically exclude flood.

What homeowners insurance usually does cover is sudden, accidental water damage from inside the home, like a burst supply line or a water heater that fails. If a sump pump backs up or a sewer line floods a basement, that's typically covered only with a separate water backup (sewer/sump) endorsement added to the policy, not under standard coverage. We cover this split in more detail in our guide on [storm damage insurance coverage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-storm-damage/) and in our broader [water damage restoration cost guide for Washington](/blog/water-damage-restoration-cost-wa/).

Because coverage hinges on the source of the water, documentation matters. Photos of the water line, moisture readings, and a written scope of work before demolition protect a claim whether it's going through an NFIP flood policy or a homeowners endorsement.

## What Happens During Flood Damage Restoration?

Flood restoration moves through extraction, assessment, controlled demolition, structural drying, and rebuild, usually in that order over several days to a few weeks depending on scope.

1. **Water extraction.** Pumps and truck-mounted extractors remove standing water as fast as possible; every hour water sits increases the chance materials can't be saved.
2. **Moisture mapping and category assessment.** A moisture meter and thermal imaging identify how far water traveled behind walls and under flooring, and the water is classified by category to determine the right drying and sanitation approach.
3. **Controlled demolition.** Wet insulation, ruined carpet and pad, and drywall that's wicked water above the flood cut line are removed to stop ongoing damage and allow the cavity to dry.
4. **Structural drying.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run until the structure reaches a dry standard consistent with the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), typically monitored daily with moisture readings.
5. **Rebuild.** Drywall, insulation, flooring, trim, and paint go back in once the space tests dry. Mold risk rises fast on wet materials, generally within 24 to 48 hours per the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home), so this timeline matters as much as the cost.
