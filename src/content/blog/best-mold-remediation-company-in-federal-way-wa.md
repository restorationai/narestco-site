## What should you actually check before hiring a mold remediation company?

Check three things before signing anything: the containment method they describe, whether they test after the work instead of just before it, and whether their estimate is a written scope, not a verbal guess. A company that can't describe how it will isolate the work area from the rest of the house is skipping a basic step in the [ANSI/IICRC S520 Standard for Professional Mold Remediation](https://iicrc.org/s520/).

Ask specifically what happens to porous materials like drywall and insulation that touched the mold. The S520 standard generally calls for removing porous materials with heavy colonization rather than trying to clean and seal them in place, because cleaning alone often leaves spores embedded in the material. If a company proposes spraying and painting over visibly colonized drywall without removing it, get a second opinion.

If you're not sure you even have a mold problem yet, versus just musty air or a water stain, our guide on [signs of hidden mold](/blog/signs-of-hidden-mold/) walks through the clues worth checking before you call anyone.

## What does mold remediation actually involve?

Mold remediation is the physical process of containing, removing, and verifying the removal of mold growth, as distinct from mold inspection, which just identifies and documents where it is. A remediation job typically runs through five phases: containment setup, source correction, removal of affected materials, HEPA-filtered air scrubbing, and a post-remediation verification check.

Containment comes first: poly sheeting and negative air pressure keep spores from spreading to clean parts of the home while work is underway. Technicians in full Tyvek suits and respirators then remove affected porous materials and HEPA-vacuum the remaining surfaces. An air scrubber runs through the containment zone to pull airborne spores out of circulation.

The job isn't finished until the moisture source causing the mold is also fixed, whether that's a crawl space vapor barrier, a roof leak, or a failed bath fan vent. The [EPA's mold cleanup guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-cleanup-your-home) is consistent on this point: mold returns if the moisture problem behind it isn't corrected, no matter how thorough the removal was.

## What should you do next?

Start with a mold inspection to confirm the extent of the problem before agreeing to a remediation scope. A written inspection report, ideally with moisture readings and a sketch of affected areas, protects you if you later need to compare bids or file an insurance claim.

Homeowners insurance typically covers mold remediation only when it results from a sudden, covered water loss, such as a burst pipe, and coverage is often capped even then; gradual leaks and long-term moisture are usually excluded. Keep photos and a copy of the inspection report regardless of how you pay for the work.
