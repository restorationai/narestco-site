## What Separates a Smoke Specialist From a General Cleaning Crew?

A smoke specialist identifies the soot type before touching a surface, because wet soot, dry soot, and protein soot each need a different cleaning agent and a different approach. A general cleaning crew that scrubs every surface the same way can smear soot deeper into porous materials or set a protein film that no amount of wiping will lift.

Dry soot comes from fast, high-heat fires (wood, paper) and wipes off relatively easily with dry chemical sponges. Wet soot comes from smoldering, low-oxygen fires (plastics, foam, synthetics) and smears into a sticky residue that needs solvent-based cleaners. Protein soot comes from kitchen fires involving food, meat, or grease, and often leaves behind a nearly invisible film with an intensely strong odor, which is why a scorched pan fire can smell far worse than the visible damage suggests.

The [ANSI/IICRC S700 Standard](https://iicrc.org/s700/) is the industry reference restoration crews are trained against for identifying soot type, selecting cleaning agents, and sequencing a fire and smoke job correctly. A crew that references this standard, rather than guessing by eye, is doing the work a specialist does.

## How Does Professional Smoke Odor Removal Actually Work?

Smoke odor removal works by physically cleaning every affected surface first, then neutralizing what's left in the air and inside porous materials with specialized equipment, not by masking the smell. Air fresheners and ozone sprays from a hardware store only cover odor temporarily because the soot particles carrying it are still embedded in drywall, fabric, and ductwork.

Professional crews typically combine thermal fogging, which drives a deodorizing agent into the same cracks and pores smoke originally penetrated, with hydroxyl or ozone generators that break down odor molecules in the air over several hours. HVAC ductwork gets cleaned or sealed separately, since a fire that burns for even a few minutes can push soot through the whole duct system and re-contaminate a room weeks after the walls look clean. ## What Should You Throw Away After Smoke Damage?

Throw away anything porous that absorbed smoke before the fire was out: open food, medications, cosmetics, and anything plastic that was near direct heat, since these can't be fully decontaminated. Soft, absorbent materials close to the fire, like pillows, some upholstered furniture, and certain carpet padding, often follow the same rule if the odor has fully penetrated them.

Hard, non-porous items (metal, glass, most sealed hard plastics, some wood furniture) can usually be cleaned and saved. Clothing and linens often come back after specialized ozone or dry-cleaning treatment rather than a home wash, which can set the odor permanently. A restoration crew doing a contents inventory before demolition starts is what keeps a homeowner from losing items that were actually salvageable.

## Will Insurance Cover Smoke Damage Restoration in Washington?

Most homeowners insurance policies cover smoke damage restoration when the fire itself was sudden and accidental, which is the typical fire scenario. The policy generally pays for cleaning, odor remediation, and repairing what the fire and smoke damaged, subject to the policy's limits and deductible.

What gets disputed is scope: whether odor has actually cleared a room, whether a duct system needs full cleaning or just a wipe-down, and whether contents are salvageable or need replacement. Detailed photo documentation and a written scope from the restoration company, submitted before demolition, is what protects that part of the claim.
