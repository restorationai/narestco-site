Once water has soaked drywall, insulation, flooring, or a crawl space, total repair costs including extraction, drying equipment, and rebuild usually run $1,500 to $18,000 or more, depending on where the pipe broke, how long it ran, and how much of the house got wet.

If you're staring at water coming through a ceiling in Steel Lake or standing over a soaked crawl space access panel in a 1970s rambler near Twin Lakes, you're probably trying to figure out two different numbers at once: what the plumber charges to fix the pipe, and what it costs to deal with everything the water touched on the way out. Those are separate bills, paid to separate people, and conflating them is the single biggest source of sticker shock after a burst pipe.

## How much does it cost to fix a burst pipe in Federal Way, WA?

Most homeowners in Federal Way pay between $150 and $1,800 just for the plumbing repair, and between $1,500 and $18,000 or more once water damage cleanup and rebuild are added in. The spread is wide because "burst pipe" covers everything from a pinhole leak caught in an hour to a split supply line that ran unnoticed overnight in a crawl space.

## What actually happens during burst pipe repair and water damage cleanup?

Burst pipe cleanup happens in a set order: stop the water, remove the standing water, dry the structure, then repair what's left. Skipping or shortening any of those steps is what turns a manageable claim into a mold problem three weeks later.

1. **Shut off the water.** The main or a localized valve stops the active loss before anyone assesses damage.
2. **Extract standing water.** Pumps and truck-mounted extractors remove bulk water from floors, carpet, and crawl spaces, usually within the first few hours.
3. **Document the loss.** Moisture readings, photos, and a written scope get recorded before demolition, which is the paperwork insurance adjusters actually want to see.
4. **Remove unsalvageable materials.** Wet insulation, swollen baseboards, and drywall beyond a certain saturation line typically get cut out rather than dried in place.
5. **Dry the structure.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run for several days, dried to the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), with daily moisture checks until readings return to normal.
6. **Repair the pipe and rebuild.** A licensed plumber fixes the actual pipe, then drywall, insulation, flooring, and paint get put back.

## What drives the price up or down?

The final number comes down to five things: where the pipe broke, how long it ran, how contaminated the water is, how much square footage got wet, and how fast someone responded.

- **Location of the break.** A pipe burst in an open basement wall is cheap to access. A pipe burst under a crawl space home, which is common in South King County's older 1970s-80s ramblers built before current vapor-barrier standards, means a technician has to work in a tight, often wet, unconditioned space, which raises labor time.
- **How long it ran.** A pipe caught within an hour wets less material than one that ran overnight while a family was at The Commons mall or asleep. Time is the single biggest cost multiplier.
- **Water category.** A clean supply-line break (Category 1 water) is cheaper to remediate than water that's been sitting and picked up contamination, which may require more material removal.
- **Square footage affected.** Water travels along floor joists and inside wall cavities, often wetting rooms well beyond where the pipe actually broke.
- **Response time.** Materials dried within 24 to 48 hours are far more likely to be saved than materials left wet longer, which affects how much gets replaced versus dried in place.

## Does homeowners insurance cover a burst pipe in Federal Way?

Most homeowners policies cover a burst pipe because it's sudden and accidental damage, but they typically exclude damage from a leak that was gradual or caused by poor maintenance. The distinction between "sudden burst" and "slow leak" is usually what decides whether a claim gets paid.

A supply line that splits overnight is the kind of loss most standard policies are built to cover. A pipe that's been weeping slowly for months, by contrast, often gets denied as a maintenance issue. Homeowners also have a duty to mitigate: documenting the damage with photos and starting water removal promptly protects the claim, while letting water sit can give an adjuster grounds to push back. For general coverage patterns, the Insurance Information Institute has a plain-language breakdown of [what standard homeowners insurance covers](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance), and Washington residents can check policy specifics through the [Washington Office of the Insurance Commissioner](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/).

If the pipe in question runs under your foundation rather than through a wall or crawl space, coverage rules shift, and we've written a separate guide on [whether homeowners insurance covers broken pipes under a foundation](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-broken-pipes-under-foundation/) that's worth reading before you file.
