When a loss spreads across multiple floors, units, or buildings, the standard playbook stops working. A single apartment complex with water intrusion on three floors, a commercial building with fire damage that compromised structural framing, or a storm event that hit a dozen properties at once all need something different: a response built around parallel crews, a dedicated project manager, and documentation detailed enough to support a claim that may run into six or seven figures. That's the gap large loss and catastrophic response is built to close.

## What Large Loss and Catastrophic Response actually involves

Industry-wide, a loss is typically classified as "large" once it crosses roughly $50,000 to $100,000 in anticipated restoration and reconstruction costs, or once it involves multiple units, structures, or a commercial operation with business interruption exposure. At that scale, the work isn't just bigger, it's structured differently. Instead of one crew working a linear path, you need several crews running extraction, demolition, and drying simultaneously in different zones, backed by enough desiccant dehumidification capacity, air movers, and generator power to cover the footprint without bottlenecking. National Restoration Construction holds a General Contractor Certificate of Registration, which matters here because large loss jobs almost always carry into reconstruction, structural repair, and multi-trade coordination, not just mitigation.

## Our process

1. **Rapid-scale mobilization and triage.** Crews and equipment are staged across the full affected footprint at once, with an initial safety and structural walkthrough to flag compromised framing, electrical hazards, or areas needing engineering review before anyone enters.
2. **Full-scope documentation.** Moisture mapping, photographic logs, and affected-material inventories are built out unit by unit or zone by zone, producing a line-item scope that matches the detail carriers and third-party administrators expect on a large claim.
3. **Parallel mitigation across trades.** Extraction, containment, and demolition run concurrently in separate zones instead of sequentially, which is the difference between a four-week mitigation phase and a four-month one on a multi-structure loss.
4. **Dedicated project management.** One point of contact tracks daily progress logs, psychrometric readings, and equipment run times, and coordinates walk-throughs with adjusters so the paper trail keeps pace with the physical work.
5. **Reconstruction handoff.** Once drying and demolition are verified complete, the same team carries the job into permitting, framing, and finish work rather than handing it to a separate contractor who has to re-learn the scope.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The most common failure on large claims is fragmentation: a mitigation company handles the water or fire portion, then disappears, and a general contractor starts reconstruction weeks later without ever seeing the original moisture logs. That gap is where disputes with carriers happen, because the documentation doesn't connect. A good operator keeps one continuous record from first extraction to final punch list, including daily drying logs per zone (not just an average for the building), photos tied to specific units or areas, and an estimate format that lines up with what adjusters and TPAs are already using to process the claim. Under-scoping is the other recurring problem: crews that treat a 40,000 square foot loss like a scaled-up residential job tend to miss hidden moisture in multi-layer assemblies, shared wall cavities, or HVAC systems that carried contamination between units. Structural components affected by fire or prolonged water exposure should get an engineer's review before reconstruction begins, not an assumption that framing is sound because it's still standing.

Large loss projects don't price like standard residential claims, so industry ranges below are rough and meant only to set expectations, not a quote. ## Seasonal and regional considerations

Federal Way and the broader Puget Sound region see large loss events most often tied to atmospheric river storms and the windstorms that follow them, which can cause roof failures, wind-driven water intrusion, and tree damage across several buildings on a single property or HOA in the same event. Older multi-family stock built before modern stormwater and roofing codes is more exposed to these cascading failures, which is often why a single storm turns into a true large loss rather than a handful of isolated claims.
