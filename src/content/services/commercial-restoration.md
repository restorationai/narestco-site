A burst sprinkler head over a retail sales floor on a Friday night, a kitchen fire that shuts down a restaurant mid-lease, a flat commercial roof that lets a storm through into a suite of offices: each of these stops a business from operating, not just a structure from looking right. Commercial restoration deals with the second clock that residential jobs don't have, the one counting lost revenue, lease obligations, and tenant complaints while the drying equipment runs.

## What Commercial Restoration actually involves

Commercial loss work covers office suites, retail storefronts, warehouses, restaurants, and multi-tenant buildings, and the scope is shaped as much by the building's use as by the damage itself. A water loss in a dentist's office means protecting equipment and patient records differently than a water loss in a warehouse full of palletized inventory. Commercial-grade extraction units, desiccant dehumidifiers, and air movers are sized to square footage that often runs into the thousands, and drying plans account for slab-on-grade construction, suspended ceilings, and HVAC systems that can spread contamination if they're not isolated early. Timelines depend on category of damage and how much of the space has to stay operational during the work; a single-suite water loss might dry in days, while a fire loss involving contents, electrical, and structural repairs can run weeks.

## Our process

1. **Loss assessment and scope documentation.** We walk the affected space with property management or ownership, document pre-loss conditions where possible, and build a scope that insurance adjusters and lenders can follow.
2. **Site stabilization.** Board-up, tarping, power isolation, and water shutoff happen first so the loss doesn't spread into adjacent units or trigger a second claim.
3. **Mitigation specific to the loss type.** Water losses get extraction, structural drying, and moisture monitoring; fire losses get soot characterization, content assessment, and odor control; each runs on its own equipment and timeline.
4. **Phased reconstruction.** Where possible, we sequence repairs so part of the building can reopen while work continues in the affected section, which matters for tenants who can't afford a full closure.
5. **Final walkthrough and documentation.** Moisture readings, photos, and a completed scope go to the owner and the carrier so the claim file closes cleanly.

## What separates a good commercial restoration response from a bad one

The biggest technical miss on commercial jobs is treating a large space like a scaled-up residential one. A 5,000-square-foot office floor doesn't dry on the same equipment ratio as a bedroom, and under-equipping a job is how a water loss turns into a mold claim six weeks later. Moisture behind commercial wall assemblies, especially metal stud with vapor barrier, can trap water in ways that read dry at the surface, so monitoring has to go deeper than a visual check. On the claims side, adjusters want documentation of pre-existing conditions, clear photos tied to a floor plan, and a scope that separates mitigation from reconstruction, because those often get paid under different coverage lines. A good response also accounts for tenants: coordinating after-hours work, dust containment for occupied adjacent units, and a realistic reopening date instead of an optimistic one.

## What does Commercial Restoration cost?

Commercial restoration costs vary widely based on square footage, occupancy type, and whether the business needs to stay partially open during the work. These figures are typical industry ranges, not a quote, since every loss is different and National Restoration Construction provides a written scope before work begins.

| Scenario | Typical range |
|---|---|

Most commercial property policies cover sudden water and fire losses along with some business interruption costs, though coverage for gradual damage or deferred maintenance issues is often excluded, so it's worth reading the policy language before assuming what's included.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Federal Way's flat-roofed retail and warehouse stock takes the brunt of the region's wet season, when atmospheric river storms from November through February put sustained rain on roofing systems that weren't built for standing water. Commercial buildings with reduced weekend heating are also more exposed to pipe freezes during the occasional hard cold snap, since a vacant building loses heat faster than an occupied one. Buildings near Puget Sound and the lower Green River valley see higher ambient humidity most of the year, which slows structural drying timelines compared to drier inland climates.
