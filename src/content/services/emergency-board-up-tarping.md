## What Emergency Board-Up and Tarping actually involves

Board-up and tarping is temporary structural protection, not a repair. It covers broken windows, kicked-in doors, fire-damaged walls, and roof sections opened up by a falling tree limb, wind-driven debris, or fire department ventilation during suppression. Crews use exterior-grade plywood, heavy-mil reinforced poly sheeting, furring strips, and construction-grade fasteners rated to hold against sustained wind, not the thin tarps and duct tape sold at hardware stores. Roof tarping typically involves a peak-to-eave shingle fold or batten-down method so wind can't get under the edge and peel it off overnight. The goal is a seal that survives the days or weeks between the loss event and permanent reconstruction, not just the next few hours.

## Our process

1. **Site safety check.** Before anyone climbs a roof or pulls broken glass, the crew checks for electrical hazards, structural instability, and sharp debris, especially important after fire damage where char can hide weakened framing.
2. **Measure and seal the opening.** Windows, doors, and wall breaches get measured and cut to fit, then fastened with construction screws rather than nails so boards hold through wind events and come off cleanly later without tearing trim.
3. **Roof tarping.** Damaged roof sections are covered with reinforced poly, lapped correctly at seams, and battened down along the perimeter so wind can't lift an edge. On steep or multi-story roofs this is done with fall-protection gear.
4. **Photo documentation.** Every boarded opening and tarped section is photographed before and after for the insurance file, with notes on the cause of the opening (storm, fire, break-in) since that detail affects how a claim gets coded.
5. **Scope handoff.** Once the structure is secure, our team puts together a written scope for the permanent repair, whether that's water extraction, fire and soot cleanup, or reconstruction, so there's no gap between stabilization and the real fix.

## What separates a good board-up and tarping response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating board-up as a five-minute afterthought. Plywood nailed directly into painted siding or trim without furring strips causes cosmetic damage that becomes a separate line item on the repair estimate. Tarps stretched tight without slack for water runoff pond rainwater on the roof deck instead of shedding it, which can soak through at the first seam failure. Boards cut slightly undersize leave gaps that rain and wind find within a day. Adjusters also look for whether the mitigation was documented at the time, not reconstructed from memory weeks later, so photos and a dated scope matter as much as the physical work itself.
