## Restoration emergencies common in Pacific

Pacific sits in the White River valley on the King-Pierce county line, low ground that has flooded historically and still holds water during a hard winter storm or a fast snowmelt. Heavy Pacific Northwest rain finds its way into crawlspaces and along foundation walls faster here than it does on higher ground nearby, and sump pumps that haven't been serviced in a few years tend to fail at exactly the wrong moment. Add in the area's older housing stock, where galvanized supply lines and aging water heaters are still common, and burst-pipe calls cluster right alongside storm-driven flooding. Wildfire smoke drift during late-summer dry spells is also a recurring reason we get calls about smoke odor in homes that never saw a flame.

## Services we provide in Pacific

Water damage in Pacific often starts below the floor, not above it. Crawlspace intrusion from saturated soil or a cracked supply line can sit unnoticed for days before it surfaces as warped flooring or a musty smell, so our process includes moisture mapping under the structure, not just the visible room.

Fire and smoke damage work here frequently involves clearing soot and odor from older plaster and lath assemblies that hold residue differently than modern drywall, along with content cleaning for smoke-affected belongings.

Mold remediation follows naturally from the valley's humidity and flood history. Once moisture sits in a wall cavity or crawlspace for more than a day or two, colonization starts, and containment plus controlled drying matters more than scrubbing visible growth.

Storm damage and emergency board-up or tarping round out our most-requested work after wind events, protecting a damaged roof or broken window until permanent repairs can start.

Biohazard and trauma cleanup, and the reconstruction that follows any of the above, are handled by the same crew so property owners aren't coordinating three different contractors mid-crisis.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Pacific

Much of Pacific's housing stock reflects the broader Green River valley pattern: older single-story homes on crawlspaces mixed with newer construction on slab, with supply plumbing ranging from galvanized steel in older houses to copper and PEX in anything remodeled or built more recently. Galvanized lines corrode from the inside and tend to fail at joints and elbows, which is where we see the slow, hidden leaks that do the most damage before anyone notices. Homes built before the 1980s may also carry asbestos-containing materials in flooring or pipe insulation, which changes how a demolition scope has to be handled.

The valley floor sits on alluvial soil with a naturally high water table, so crawlspaces and foundations here handle groundwater differently than homes built on higher, better-drained ground. Structural repairs and rebuilds following a serious loss go through the permitting process administered by the City of Pacific or the relevant county building department depending on exact location, and any work touching load-bearing elements or egress needs that sign-off before we close up walls.
